intellectual timeline

Ongoing — last updated May 2026

A working timeline to contextualize the ideas, texts, and events that keep appearing across these notes. Figures are placed at the dates of their major works; historical events are placed where they intersect the intellectual history.

Marx Weber Gramsci World Wars Rural Realism

1818

Marx Karl Marx born in Trier, Prussia.

1845 – 1848

Marx Theses on Feuerbach (1845); The German Ideology with Engels (1845–46, unpublished until 1932). The foundational break with Hegelian idealism — materialism as method. The Communist Manifesto published 1848, the same year as the wave of European revolutions.

1860 – 1861

Rural George Eliot — The Mill on the Floss (1860), Silas Marner (1861). Rural England depicted as socially merciless; community judgment functions as punishment.

1864

Weber Max Weber born in Erfurt, Prussia.

1867

Marx Das Kapital, Vol. I. The analysis of commodity fetishism, surplus value, and the hidden extraction underlying capitalist production.

1871

Marx The Paris Commune. The first experiment in workers' self-governance, crushed after 72 days. Marx analyzes it in The Civil War in France. Shapes Marx's thinking on the state and revolution in his final years.

1874 – 1895

Rural Thomas Hardy — the Wessex novels. Far from the Madding Crowd (1874) · The Return of the Native (1878) · Tess of the d'Urbervilles (1891) · Jude the Obscure (1895). Nature is beautiful and indifferent; social convention destroys individuals who transgress it.

1883

Marx Marx dies in London. Das Kapital Vols. II and III edited and published posthumously by Engels.

1887

Rural Émile Zola — La Terre. The sharpest entry in the tradition; a documentary corrective to George Sand's idealized rural fiction. Peasant life depicted as violent, grasping, and economically brutal.

1891

Gramsci Antonio Gramsci born in Ales, Sardinia — rural, impoverished, peripheral. His origin shapes his later thinking about the south of Italy and uneven development.

1904 – 1905

Weber The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. The argument that Calvinist theology produced the psychological conditions for capitalist accumulation — worldly asceticism, the calling, the proof of salvation through work. A deliberate conversation with Marx: Weber agrees capitalism is a system but disputes that its origins are purely material.

1913

Rural Willa Cather — O Pioneers! The Great Plains as a site of immigrant labor, failure, and ambiguous elegy. Refuses the triumphalism American frontier mythology demanded.

1914 – 1918

World War I

July 1914: assassination of Franz Ferdinand triggers alliance cascade. 1917: Russian Revolution; US enters. November 1918: Armistice. ~20 million dead. The war destroys the liberal optimism of the 19th century and produces the conditions for both fascism and the Soviet experiment.

Gramsci Gramsci becomes editor of the socialist newspaper Avanti! in Turin. The war radicalizes the Italian left and sets the stage for factory occupations and the Biennio Rosso (1919–20).

1918 – 1919

Weber Politics as a Vocation and Science as a Vocation (lectures, 1917–19). On the disenchantment of the world, the rationalization of all spheres of life, and the bureaucratic iron cage. Weber sees no exit.
Rural Sherwood Anderson — Winesburg, Ohio. Small-town rural America as a space of psychological isolation and failed communication.

1920

Weber Weber dies in Munich, aged 56, of pneumonia during the Spanish flu. Economy and Society published posthumously (1922) — his major theoretical synthesis.

1921 – 1926

Gramsci Co-founds the Italian Communist Party (1921). Elected to parliament (1924). Mussolini consolidates power and strips parliamentary immunity. Gramsci arrested November 1926. At his trial, the prosecutor reportedly said: "We must stop this brain from functioning for twenty years."

1929 – 1935

Gramsci Gramsci writes the Prison Notebooks from his cell at Turi di Bari. ~3,000 pages on hegemony, civil society, the role of intellectuals, and why revolution had failed in the West. Written under censorship, in deliberately indirect language. The concept of hegemony — that ruling classes govern through consent, not just coercion — is developed here.

1937

Gramsci Gramsci dies in a Rome clinic, 46 years old. He had spent eleven years in Fascist prisons. The Prison Notebooks are smuggled out by his sister-in-law Tatiana and eventually published from 1947 onward.

1939 – 1945

World War II

September 1939: Germany invades Poland. 1940: Fall of France. 1941: Germany invades USSR; Japan attacks Pearl Harbor. 1944: D-Day. May 1945: Germany surrenders. August 1945: Japan surrenders after atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. ~70–85 million dead, the majority civilians. The war produces the conditions for decolonization, the Cold War, and the postwar welfare state.

Rural John Steinbeck — The Grapes of Wrath (1939). Rural realism as political argument: the Joads are destroyed not by nature or social convention but by an economic system that treats agricultural labor as disposable.

1960s – 1990

Rural John McGahern — the Leitrim novels and stories. Rural Catholic Ireland rendered in prose of extraordinary restraint. Amongst Women (1990) is his masterpiece: patriarchal authority in slow decline on a small farm.

1976

Rural Inès Cagnati — Crazy Genie. Narrated by a young girl about her ostracized mother, a farmhand in rural France cast out for having a child out of wedlock. Simple, poetic, repetitive prose. Poverty, prejudice, and fierce maternal love without resolution.

1983

Rural Annie Ernaux — La Place. Rural realism shading into autofiction: an account of her father and the class distance her education opened between them. "I tried to gather together the words, the phrases, the events that were the marks of both a culture and an impoverishment."
