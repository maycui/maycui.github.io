contextual timeline

A working timeline to contextualize the ideas, texts, and events that keep appearing across these notes. Figures are placed at the dates of their major works; historical events are placed where they intersect the intellectual history.

Global Events Sociology Literature

1588

Sociology Thomas Hobbes born in Westport, Wiltshire, England. Born prematurely, reportedly when his mother went into labour on hearing of the Spanish Armada approaching. He later joked that "fear and I were born twins."

1632

Sociology John Locke born in Wrington, Somerset, England.

1642 – 1651

English Civil War

Parliament raises an army against King Charles I. 1649: Charles I is tried and publicly executed — the first time a reigning English monarch is put to death by his own subjects. England enters a decade of military rule under Oliver Cromwell. The established order collapses entirely. Hobbes, watching from exile in Paris, writes Leviathan in direct response: without a sovereign, this is what happens.

1651

Sociology Hobbes — Leviathan. The foundational argument for absolute sovereign authority: without it, life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. Written in exile in Paris as a direct response to the chaos of the Civil War.

1679

Sociology Hobbes dies in Hardwick, aged 91. He outlived the Civil War, the Interregnum, and the Restoration, and remained intellectually active into his eighties.

1688

The Glorious Revolution

James II is deposed without bloodshed and replaced by William and Mary under explicit constitutional conditions — a Bill of Rights limiting royal power. Locke had been so closely associated with opposition to James that he had fled to the Netherlands for his safety. The revolution is, in effect, a vindication of everything Locke was arguing: a king removed by consent, constitutional limits imposed, and the country does not collapse. He publishes the Two Treatises the following year.

1689

Sociology Locke — Two Treatises of Government. Government derives legitimacy from the consent of the governed and exists to protect natural rights — life, liberty, and property. If it fails, revolution is justified. Published the year after the Glorious Revolution, partly as its retrospective justification. The American founders read Locke obsessively.

1704

Sociology Locke dies in Essex, aged 72. His ideas on natural rights, consent, and the right to revolution will travel directly into the American Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789).

1712

Sociology Jean-Jacques Rousseau born in Geneva, to a modest family. His origins — peripheral, relatively poor, outside the French establishment — shape his lifelong sensitivity to inequality and social humiliation.

1755

Sociology Rousseau — Discourse on Inequality. Private property is not a natural right but the original source of inequality: "The first man who enclosed a piece of ground and said This is mine was the real founder of civil society." A direct counter to Locke.

1762

Sociology Rousseau — The Social Contract. "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." Legitimate society must express the general will — the collective good of the whole people — not the interests of the powerful. The book is banned in Paris and Geneva. Rousseau is forced to flee. His ideas will fuel the French Revolution twenty-seven years later.

1778

Sociology Rousseau dies in Ermenonville, aged 66 — eleven years before the Revolution his ideas helped ignite. He is reburied in the Panthéon in 1794, alongside Voltaire.

1775 – 1783

American Revolution

1775: Battles of Lexington and Concord begin armed conflict with Britain. 1776: Declaration of Independence, asserting natural rights and government by consent of the governed — drawing directly on Locke and Enlightenment liberalism. 1781: British surrender at Yorktown. 1783: Treaty of Paris. The revolution produces the first modern republic built on written constitutional principles. For European radicals, it demonstrates that an existing order can be overthrown and replaced by deliberate design — a template the French will radicalize within a decade.

1789 – 1799

French Revolution

1789: Storming of the Bastille; Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen. 1792: First French Republic declared. 1793–94: The Reign of Terror under Robespierre — revolutionary logic consuming itself. 1799: Napoleon's coup (18 Brumaire) ends the revolutionary period. The Revolution destroys the feudal aristocratic order in France and sends shockwaves across Europe. It is the central political event for the generation that produces Marx and Hegel: proof that history can rupture, and that the rupture can go wrong. Marx will call it the first great bourgeois revolution — the class that overthrew the aristocracy would itself become the next ruling class to be overthrown.

c. 1760 – 1840

Industrial Revolution

Britain first. James Watt's steam engine (1769), the spinning jenny (1764), and the mechanized loom transform production from cottage craft to factory system. By 1800, Manchester is a city of mills. Railways spread across Britain in the 1830s–40s. The shift displaces agricultural labor, concentrates workers into cities, and creates the industrial proletariat that Marx will analyze. The countryside is depopulated; the pastoral ideal becomes a reaction to what urbanization destroys.

1818

Sociology Karl Marx born in Trier, Prussia.

1845 – 1848

Sociology Theses on Feuerbach (1845); The German Ideology with Engels (1845–46, unpublished until 1932). The foundational break with Hegelian idealism — materialism as method. The Communist Manifesto published 1848, the same year as the wave of European revolutions.

1864

Sociology Max Weber born in Erfurt, Prussia.

1867

Sociology Das Kapital, Vol. I. The analysis of commodity fetishism, surplus value, and the hidden extraction underlying capitalist production.

1871

Sociology The Paris Commune. The first experiment in workers' self-governance, crushed after 72 days. Marx analyzes it in The Civil War in France. Shapes Marx's thinking on the state and revolution in his final years.

1883

Sociology Marx dies in London. Das Kapital Vols. II and III edited and published posthumously by Engels.

1891

Sociology Antonio Gramsci born in Ales, Sardinia — rural, impoverished, peripheral. His origin shapes his later thinking about the south of Italy and uneven development.

1904 – 1905

Sociology The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. The argument that Calvinist theology produced the psychological conditions for capitalist accumulation — worldly asceticism, the calling, the proof of salvation through work. A deliberate conversation with Marx: Weber agrees capitalism is a system but disputes that its origins are purely material.

1914 – 1918

World War I

July 1914: assassination of Franz Ferdinand triggers alliance cascade. 1917: Russian Revolution; US enters. November 1918: Armistice. ~20 million dead. The war destroys the liberal optimism of the 19th century and produces the conditions for both fascism and the Soviet experiment.

Sociology Gramsci becomes editor of the socialist newspaper Avanti! in Turin. The war radicalizes the Italian left and sets the stage for factory occupations and the Biennio Rosso (1919–20).

1918 – 1919

Sociology Politics as a Vocation and Science as a Vocation (lectures, 1917–19). On the disenchantment of the world, the rationalization of all spheres of life, and the bureaucratic iron cage. Weber sees no exit.

1920

Sociology Weber dies in Munich, aged 56, of pneumonia during the Spanish flu. Economy and Society published posthumously (1922) — his major theoretical synthesis.

1921 – 1926

Sociology Co-founds the Italian Communist Party (1921). Elected to parliament (1924). Mussolini consolidates power and strips parliamentary immunity. Gramsci arrested November 1926. At his trial, the prosecutor reportedly said: "We must stop this brain from functioning for twenty years."

1929 – 1935

Sociology Gramsci writes the Prison Notebooks from his cell at Turi di Bari. ~3,000 pages on hegemony, civil society, the role of intellectuals, and why revolution had failed in the West. Written under censorship, in deliberately indirect language. The concept of hegemony — that ruling classes govern through consent, not just coercion — is developed here.

1937

Sociology Gramsci dies in a Rome clinic, 46 years old. He had spent eleven years in Fascist prisons. The Prison Notebooks are smuggled out by his sister-in-law Tatiana and eventually published from 1947 onward.

1939 – 1945

World War II

September 1939: Germany invades Poland. 1940: Fall of France. 1941: Germany invades USSR; Japan attacks Pearl Harbor. 1944: D-Day. May 1945: Germany surrenders. August 1945: Japan surrenders after atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. ~70–85 million dead, the majority civilians. The war produces the conditions for decolonization, the Cold War, and the postwar welfare state.

Literature John Steinbeck — The Grapes of Wrath (1939). Rural realism as political argument: the Joads are destroyed not by nature or social convention but by an economic system that treats agricultural labor as disposable.

1976

Literature Inès Cagnati — Crazy Genie. Narrated by a young girl about her ostracized mother, a farmhand in rural France cast out for having a child out of wedlock. Simple, poetic, repetitive prose. Poverty, prejudice, and fierce maternal love without resolution.
