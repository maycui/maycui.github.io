Capitalism does not announce itself. It does not present itself as one possible arrangement among many, chosen at a particular historical moment by particular people with particular interests. It presents itself as the way things are — as natural, inevitable, and just. Understanding how it does this has been one of the central problems of social theory for two centuries. Two entirely different diagnoses have been offered, arriving at the same symptom from opposite directions.

Marx — the hidden crime

Marx's starting point was that the ideas through which a society understands itself are not neutral. They are produced by the same social relations that produce everything else — and they serve the interests of those who dominate those relations.

The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, i.e. the class which is the ruling material force of society, is at the same time its ruling intellectual force. The class which has the means of material production at its disposal has control at the same time over the means of mental production.
Marx & Engels — The German Ideology, 1846

This is mystification: the process by which historically contingent social arrangements come to appear natural, inevitable, and just. It is not a conspiracy. It is not deliberate propaganda managed from above. It is structural — produced automatically by the social relations themselves, in the same way that a lens distorts without intending to.

The clearest example Marx gave was the wage relation. Under feudalism, exploitation was visible. The serf knew exactly how many days per week they worked for themselves and how many for the lord. The extraction was legible. Under capitalism, this legibility disappears. The worker and the employer appear to meet as equals — a free contract freely signed. Marx's argument was that this appearance, though not false in its own terms, conceals the decisive fact.

The value of labour-power, and the value which that labour-power creates in the labour-process, are two entirely different magnitudes; and this difference of the two values was what the capitalist had in view when he was purchasing the labour-power... The seller of labour-power, like the seller of any other commodity, realises its exchange-value, and parts with its use-value.
Marx — Capital, Vol. I, 1867

The worker is paid for their labour-power — their capacity to work — not for the actual labour they perform. The difference between the two, surplus value, is appropriated as profit. The wage form hides this. It makes a relationship of extraction look like a relationship of exchange. Under feudalism, exploitation was worn openly. Under capitalism, it is laundered through the wage.

Marx extended this analysis into the commodity itself. The product of human labour, once sold on the market, appears to have its value as an intrinsic, objective property — not as a social relation crystallized in a thing.

A commodity appears at first sight an extremely obvious, trivial thing. But its analysis brings out that it is a very strange thing, abounding in metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties... The mysterious character of the commodity-form consists therefore simply in the fact that the commodity reflects the social characteristics of men's own labour as objective characteristics of the products of labour themselves, as the socio-natural properties of these things.
Marx — Capital, Vol. I, 1867

He called this commodity fetishism: the inversion by which social relations between people appear as relations between things. The price of a good seems to belong to it the way its weight does — as a natural property, not a social one.

Lukács — phantom objectivity

Georg Lukács, writing in 1923, took Marx's commodity fetishism and extended it into a general theory of consciousness under capitalism. Reification — Verdinglichung, thing-ification — is the process by which all social relations come to appear as relations between things, governed by laws as impersonal and objective as the laws of nature.

A relation between people takes on the character of a thing and thus acquires a ‘phantom objectivity’, an autonomy that seems so strictly rational and all-embracing as to conceal every trace of its fundamental nature: the relation between people.
Lukács — History and Class Consciousness, 1923

The market does not feel like a human institution. It feels like weather. Supply and demand seem as indifferent to human wishes as gravity. This is not a misperception that could be corrected by pointing it out — it is a structural feature of how capitalism organizes experience. The phantom objectivity is real as a social fact, even if its origins are human and its form is historical.

Weber — the iron cage

Weber arrived at the same phenomenon from an entirely different direction. Where Marx said capitalism feels natural because it hides something — conceals exploitation behind the appearance of free exchange — Weber said it feels inescapable because it has emptied the world of everything that might judge or resist it.

The argument began with the Protestant ethic. Weber traced the cultural origins of capitalism's spirit to Calvinist theology: the idea that worldly success might be a sign of divine election. This gave tireless economic activity a religious meaning. But the religious scaffolding eventually collapsed, leaving only the activity.

The idea of duty in one’s calling prowls about in our lives like the ghost of dead religious beliefs.
Weber — The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, 1905

What remained once the transcendent meaning drained away was what Weber called the iron cage: a rational economic order so thoroughly organized by bureaucratic efficiency that it no longer required the values that had created it, and could no longer be evaluated by anything outside itself.

The Puritan wanted to work in a calling; we are forced to do so. For when asceticism was carried out of monastic cells into everyday life, and began to dominate worldly morality, it did its part in building the tremendous cosmos of the modern economic order. This order is now bound to the technical and economic conditions of machine production which today determine the lives of all the individuals who are born into this mechanism, not only those directly concerned with economic acquisition, with irresistible force.
Weber — The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, 1905
The fate of our times is characterized by rationalization and intellectualization and, above all, by the disenchantment of the world. Precisely the ultimate and most sublime values have retreated from public life either into the transcendent realm of mystic life or into the brotherliness of direct and personal human relations.
Weber — Science as a Vocation, 1917

Disenchantment is not mystification. Marx's problem was false appearance — things look more fixed and natural than they are. Weber's problem was the opposite: things have been stripped of every meaning that might make them feel contestable. There is no God, no tradition, no community obligation left standing outside the economic order that could serve as a vantage point for judging it. Capitalism does not need to deceive anyone. It simply occupies a space from which everything else has been evicted.

Marx — mystification

Capitalism conceals its own nature. The wage relation appears as fair exchange; the commodity appears to have value as a natural property. The problem is false appearance.

Solution: see through. Strip away the appearance and recover the real social relation underneath.

Weber — disenchantment

Capitalism has emptied the world of the values that might resist it. God, tradition, community obligation — all dissolved. There is nothing left outside the cage to appeal to.

Solution: none offered. The cage may be permanent. At best, new prophets. At worst, specialists without spirit.

Adorno & Horkheimer — both at once

The Frankfurt School's most important move was to refuse the choice between these two diagnoses. Adorno and Horkheimer, writing in California exile in 1944 while watching Hollywood and listening to jazz radio, argued that Enlightenment instrumental reason simultaneously mystifies its own domination and destroys the values that could resist it. Both things are happening at the same time.

Enlightenment, understood in the widest sense as the advance of thought, has always aimed at liberating human beings from fear and installing them as masters. Yet the fully enlightened earth radiates disaster triumphant.
Adorno & Horkheimer — Dialectic of Enlightenment, 1944

The culture industry extends this into daily life. Mass entertainment does not force compliance — it manufactures it, shaping desire so that what people want is precisely what the system offers. Resistance cannot find a foothold because the terrain on which resistance would need to stand has been colonized by the thing being resisted.

The culture industry perpetually cheats its consumers of what it perpetually promises. The promissory note which, with its plots and staging, it draws on pleasure is endlessly prolonged; the promise, which is actually all the spectacle consists of, is illusory: all it actually confirms is that the real point will never be reached.
Adorno & Horkheimer — Dialectic of Enlightenment, 1944
The synthesis

Marx showed that capitalism hides what it is. Weber showed that capitalism has destroyed the vocabulary by which it might be named. Adorno and Horkheimer showed that these are not two separate problems but one: instrumental reason mystifies its own operation by means of the disenchantment that makes critical distance impossible. The system conceals itself through the same process that makes concealment unnecessary.

Althusser — you are called into being

Louis Althusser pushed the analysis further into the structure of subjectivity itself. Ideology, for Althusser, is not primarily a set of false beliefs that people hold. It is the medium through which individuals are constituted as subjects — through which they come to recognize themselves as the kind of person who belongs in this society, who has this kind of life, who wants these kinds of things.

Ideology represents the imaginary relationship of individuals to their real conditions of existence.
Althusser — Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses, 1970

The mechanism Althusser named for this was interpellation — hailing. Ideology recruits individuals as subjects by calling to them, and they recognize themselves in the call.

Ideology ‘acts’ or ‘functions’ in such a way that it ‘recruits’ subjects among the individuals (it recruits them all), or ‘transforms’ the individuals into subjects (it transforms them all) by that very precise operation which I have called interpellation or hailing, and which can be imagined along the lines of the most commonplace everyday police (or other) hailing: ‘Hey, you there!’
Althusser — Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses, 1970

When you turn around in response to being hailed, you have already accepted the terms of the address. You have recognized yourself as the person being spoken to. For Althusser, capitalist society perpetually hails its members into the identities — worker, consumer, citizen, individual — that reproduce its conditions. You do not adopt these identities consciously. They are the water you swim in before you can ask what water is.

Bourdieu — the body knows

Pierre Bourdieu brought the analysis to its deepest register. Where Marx located naturalization in ideology, and Althusser in subjectivity, Bourdieu located it in the body itself. The habitus — the system of durable dispositions that structure perception, thought, and action — is social structure made flesh. It is not what you believe. It is how you move, what you notice, what strikes you as possible or absurd, who you feel comfortable around.

The conditionings associated with a particular class of conditions of existence produce habitus, systems of durable, transposable dispositions, structured structures predisposed to function as structuring structures, that is, as principles which generate and organize practices and representations that can be objectively adapted to their outcomes without presupposing a conscious aiming at ends or an express mastery of the operations necessary in order to attain them.
Bourdieu — The Logic of Practice, 1980

Capitalism feels natural not only because it hides its operations, not only because it has emptied the world of alternatives, not only because it constitutes you as a subject. It feels natural because it has reorganized your nervous system. Your body has internalized the social order as its own spontaneous inclinations.

The body believes in what it plays at: it weeps if it mimes grief. It does not represent what it performs, it does not memorize the past, it enacts the past, bringing it back to life.
Bourdieu — The Logic of Practice, 1980

The name Bourdieu gave to the deepest form of this naturalization was doxa — the unquestioned, pre-reflexive experience of the social world as self-evident. Not ideology in the sense of a set of ideas you have been persuaded to hold, but the horizon of the thinkable itself: what never needs to be said because it goes without saying.

Every established order tends to produce (to very different degrees and with very different means) the naturalization of its own arbitrariness.
Bourdieu — Outline of a Theory of Practice, 1972

And the mechanism through which this naturalization operates — through which a contingent social order imposes itself as necessary — Bourdieu called symbolic violence.

Symbolic violence is the violence which is exercised upon a social agent with his or her complicity.
Bourdieu — Pascalian Meditations, 1997

With his or her complicity. Not against a resisting subject, but through a willing one — a subject who has so thoroughly incorporated the order that they experience its impositions as their own desires, its limits as their own horizons, its arbitrary arrangements as the natural shape of things.
