foundational thoughts around modern organized societies

May 2026

The question Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau share is deceptively simple: why do we live in organized societies, and is that arrangement legitimate? Their method is also shared — imagine what humans were like before society, reason forward, and see what conclusions follow. The answers they reach are almost perfectly opposed, and between them they define the terrain of Western political philosophy for the next three centuries.

Crucially, they were not writing in a vacuum. Each was responding to the specific political crisis of their time, and understanding that context is essential to understanding why they landed where they did.

Thomas Hobbes

1588 – 1679 · Leviathan, 1651

Historical context

Hobbes lived through the English Civil War (1642–1651) — a period in which the established order collapsed entirely. Parliament raised an army against the king, Charles I was tried and publicly executed in 1649, and England lurched between military rule and constitutional experiment for a decade. Hobbes had also watched the Thirty Years War (1618–1648) devastate continental Europe in the name of religious difference, killing perhaps a third of the German population.

He was not theorizing abstractly. He had seen what happened when sovereign authority broke down. Leviathan was written in exile in Paris, partly as a justification for the strong centralized state he believed was the only alternative to chaos. His political philosophy is inseparable from the trauma of witnessing a society tear itself apart.

The life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.

Hobbes, Leviathan, 1651

Without a sovereign to enforce order, Hobbes argued, humans exist in a permanent state of war — not because they are evil, but because in conditions of scarcity and no authority, rational self-interest leads everyone to preemptive aggression. Society is the cage we built to contain ourselves. The sovereign's power must be absolute precisely because any challenge to it risks returning to the chaos Hobbes had witnessed firsthand.

The obligation of subjects to the sovereign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth by which he is able to protect them.

Hobbes, Leviathan, 1651

John Locke

1632 – 1704 · Two Treatises of Government, 1689

Historical context

Locke wrote in the shadow of a different English crisis: the Exclusion Crisis of the 1670s–80s, in which Parliament attempted to bar the Catholic James II from succeeding to the throne, and then the Glorious Revolution of 1688, in which James was deposed and replaced by William and Mary under explicit constitutional conditions. Locke had been so closely associated with opposition to James that he had fled to the Netherlands for his safety.

The Glorious Revolution was, in a sense, a vindication of everything Locke was arguing: a king had been removed by consent, a constitutional settlement imposed, and the country had not collapsed. His Two Treatises were published the following year — partly as a retrospective justification of exactly what had just happened.

Locke was also deeply shaped by the Scientific Revolution. Newton, Bacon, and the rise of empiricism were transforming how educated Europeans thought about knowledge and authority. Locke applied the same skepticism toward received political authority that scientists were applying to received cosmology.

Men being by nature all free, equal, and independent, no one can be put out of this estate and subjected to the political power of another without his own consent.

Locke, Second Treatise of Government, 1689

Where Hobbes saw the state of nature as violent chaos, Locke saw it as merely inconvenient — humans already have natural rights to life, liberty, and property, but without a common authority to adjudicate disputes, those rights are insecure. Government exists not to contain human nature but to protect rights that pre-exist it. Crucially, this means government can lose its legitimacy — and when it does, revolution is not just permissible but obligatory.

Whenever the legislators endeavor to take away and destroy the property of the people, or to reduce them to slavery under arbitrary power, they put themselves into a state of war with the people, who are thereupon absolved from any farther obedience.

Locke, Second Treatise of Government, 1689

Jean-Jacques Rousseau

1712 – 1778 · Discourse on Inequality, 1755 · The Social Contract, 1762

Historical context

Rousseau was writing in pre-Revolutionary France — the ancien régime at its most visibly contradictory: Versailles and its extravagance on one side, widespread rural poverty and a rigid feudal hierarchy on the other. The Enlightenment was in full swing, with Voltaire, Diderot, and the Encyclopédistes celebrating reason, science, and progress. Rousseau was their contemporary but became their sharpest internal critic.

He had also experienced social marginalization personally — born in Geneva to a modest family, largely self-educated, he spent years in genuine poverty and was acutely sensitive to the humiliations of class difference. Where Locke could describe property as a natural right from the relatively comfortable position of a man of means, Rousseau had lived on the wrong side of that arrangement.

His break with the Enlightenment mainstream came when he argued — scandalously, to his contemporaries — that progress and civilization were not improvements on human nature but corruptions of it. The philosophes believed reason would liberate humanity. Rousseau believed the social structures reason had built were the problem.

The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying This is mine, and found people simple enough to believe him, was the real founder of civil society.

Rousseau, Discourse on Inequality, 1755

This is a direct counter to Locke's celebration of property rights. For Rousseau, the moment private property was declared was the moment inequality became structural and permanent. All subsequent law and government were, in his view, essentially a conspiracy to protect the gains of those who had got there first.

Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains.

Rousseau, The Social Contract, 1762

Why Locke looks like the center

Placed between Hobbes and Rousseau, Locke appears as the pragmatic middle ground — accepting that we need government (Hobbes) while insisting it can be limited and removed (Rousseau). But this is partly an optical illusion: Locke looks centrist because his ideas won. Liberal constitutional democracy is essentially Lockean. From Rousseau's vantage point, Locke was already too conservative — his defense of property rights was precisely the ideology Rousseau was dismantling.
