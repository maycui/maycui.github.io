why press blue?

May 2026

The scenario

In a poll run by MrBeast, 312,448 people were given the following choice:

The rules

You vote privately — red or blue. If more than 50% vote blue, everyone survives. If fewer than 50% vote blue, only red voters survive.

44% red 312,448 votes 56% blue
Red button

You survive no matter what anyone else does. If everyone reasons this way and presses red, no one dies either. A dominant strategy with a moral escape hatch built in.

Blue button

You gamble your survival on strangers you cannot coordinate with. You survive only if the majority agrees. The irrational choice — and yet 56% made it.

The red button is not a defection that requires victims. Press red and you never die, regardless of what anyone else does. Press red and, if everyone reasons the same way, no one dies either. Given this, is there an argument to press blue?

What is Enlightenment rationalism?

To understand why the red button is the rational choice, you have to understand what rationalism, in its Enlightenment form, actually claimed. The Enlightenment — roughly the 17th and 18th centuries — was built on a radical wager: that individual human reason, properly applied, was sufficient to answer every question worth asking. You did not need tradition, scripture, or aristocratic authority. You needed only to think.

Dare to know! Have the courage to use your own understanding — that is the motto of the Enlightenment.
Kant — What is Enlightenment?, 1784

Kant's formulation — Sapere aude — captured the project in two words. The enemy was tutelage: relying on someone else to do your thinking for you. Each person, reasoning individually and correctly, would arrive at the same universal truths. Reason was not communal or situational. It was universal, abstract, and sovereign.

Descartes had laid the groundwork a century earlier. His method of radical doubt — strip away everything that could possibly be false, see what remains — produced a single irreducible starting point: cogito ergo sum. The thinking self, the reasoning individual, is the only bedrock. Everything else must be reconstructed from there.

I resolved to assume that everything that ever entered into my mind was no more true than the illusions of my dreams.
Descartes — Discourse on the Method, 1637
The key mutation

What the Enlightenment called reason was, in practice, a specific kind of reason: instrumental reason. Not "what is the good life?" but "given my goals, what is the most efficient means of achieving them?" Rationality became calculation. The moral question became a maximization problem. You reason alone, toward your own best interest, with no need to consult the community, the church, or the dead.

Adam Smith and the invisible hand

The thinker most associated with self-interest as economic principle is Adam Smith — though what Smith actually argued is considerably more careful than what gets attributed to him. The invisible hand appears exactly once in The Wealth of Nations, as a passing observation about a merchant who invests domestically for personal security and thereby promotes employment more than he intended. Smith is not claiming markets always produce optimal outcomes. He is noting that in this particular case, self-interest and public benefit happened to align.

It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. We address ourselves, not to their humanity but to their self-love.
Adam Smith — The Wealth of Nations, 1776

The point of this passage is narrower than it looks. Smith is describing how large-scale exchange among strangers works differently from exchange within a community — you cannot rely on personal affection or obligation when trading with people you will never meet, so you appeal to their interest instead. This is a sociological observation about how markets function, not a moral claim that self-interest is sufficient for social life.

Smith was, in fact, deeply suspicious of merchants and manufacturers as a class. One of his least-quoted observations cuts directly against the mythology built in his name:

People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices.
Adam Smith — The Wealth of Nations, 1776

His argument for markets was explicitly conditional. Competition must be genuine — monopoly produces extraction, not efficiency, and Smith thought the business class would pursue monopoly relentlessly. Legal institutions must exist — property rights, contract enforcement, honest courts — none of which markets produce themselves. Public goods must be publicly provided. Smith explicitly listed roads, canals, defence, justice, and public education as things markets would underprovide and that the state must supply.

He also wrote The Theory of Moral Sentiments before Wealth of Nations — a book he considered more important, built not on self-interest but on sympathy: our natural capacity to imaginatively inhabit the perspective of others. Smith never argued self-interest was sufficient for social order. He argued it was useful for coordinating large-scale exchange among strangers, within a moral and institutional framework it could not itself create.

What happened to Smith

The Smith of libertarian mythology — who said self-interest automatically produces social good and that markets should be left alone — is largely a construction of later thinkers. What survived from Smith was the stripped-down claim: private rational self-interest, left alone, produces collective good. The qualifications, the suspicion of business, the public goods arguments, the moral sentiments — these were discarded. The metaphor of the invisible hand swallowed the argument it came from. Reason privatized and sanctified — not by Smith, but in his name.

Karl Polanyi and what markets destroy

Karl Polanyi offered what may be the most important corrective to Smith. In The Great Transformation (1944), he argued that the free market was not a natural outgrowth of human nature but a historical construction — one that required the deliberate dismantling of everything that had previously constrained it. For most of human history, economic life was embedded in social relations: in kinship, community obligation, religious duty, custom. You did not maximize. You reciprocated, redistributed, and maintained standing.

The economy is submerged in social relationships. Man does not act so as to safeguard his individual interest in the possession of material goods; he acts so as to safeguard his social standing, his social claims, his social assets.
Karl Polanyi — The Great Transformation, 1944

When the market was disembedded — when land, labor, and money were treated as commodities subject to pure price logic — it created what Polanyi called a fictitious commodity system. Labor is not actually a commodity. It is human life. Land is not a commodity. It is nature and community. Treating them as such produces dislocation, atomization, and social collapse. Polanyi's core claim: the free market does not liberate social relations. It destroys them.

This matters for the red button. Smith's invisible hand promises that private rationality produces collective good automatically. Polanyi's counter is that this only appears to work because it is secretly drawing on the social fabric that pre-market communities built — and that it spends down without replenishing. Every generation raised inside market logic inherits slightly less of the cooperative instinct the system invisibly depends on. The red button is not just a thought experiment. It is a measure of how much of that inheritance remains.

The red button is the rational choice — twice over

From a purely instrumental rationalist standpoint, red is the correct answer, and it is correct in two independent ways.

I.
Individual dominance

If you press red, you survive no matter what anyone else does. If you press blue, you survive only if more than half the others also press blue — an outcome you cannot control or verify. Red is dominant. This is not a close call.

II.
The moral out

If everyone reasons as I do — and if we are all rational, they should — then everyone presses red and everyone survives. Adam Smith's invisible hand materializes in a game show. The rationalist presses red with a clean conscience.

The red button is not a thought experiment designed to make rationalism look bad. It is rationalism made visible. It is what Descartes's individual reasoning subject looks like when given a real choice with real stakes.

When rationalism eats itself: the Frankfurt School

The Frankfurt School emerged in Weimar Germany in the 1920s asking a question that rationalism could not answer from within its own resources: if reason is our guide, how did it produce this? The "this" was fascism — though what fascism actually is remains one of the most contested questions in modern political theory.

What is fascism? (the debate)

Scholars disagree on a precise definition, but most converge on several core features. Robert Paxton, whose behavioral definition is widely accepted, describes fascism as "a form of political behavior marked by obsessive preoccupation with community decline, humiliation, or victimhood and by compensatory cults of unity, energy, and purity." Roger Griffin emphasizes its ideological core: palingenetic ultranationalism — a mythic vision of national rebirth from decadence, not mere conservatism. Crucially, most scholars agree fascism is revolutionary, not reactionary — it wants to destroy the existing order and create something new through mass mobilization, violence, and the subordination of the individual to the national collective. It is anti-liberal, anti-Marxist, and anti-conservative simultaneously.

What the Frankfurt School wanted to explain was not just what fascism is, but how the society that produced Kant and Beethoven also produced Auschwitz. Their answer was deeply uncomfortable: fascism was not an aberration from modernity but a product of it — and specifically a product of the same Enlightenment rationalism that seemed to be its opposite.

The chain of reasoning runs as follows. Enlightenment reason promised liberation but mutated, over two centuries, into instrumental reason: reason that asks only "how?" and never "why?" or "whether?" Once reason is stripped of substantive moral content — once it is pure technique — it can be directed toward any end by whoever controls the apparatus. Capitalism directed it toward profit. The Nazi state directed it toward extermination. The logic was the same. Only the goal differed.

Weber had diagnosed the infrastructure this required: the rational bureaucracy. Modern states, modern armies, modern firms all depend on the same organizational form — hierarchical, rule-bound, efficient, staffed by specialists who do not ask moral questions about the system they serve. This is what made industrial-scale atrocity possible. Not hatred alone — hatred is ancient. What was new was the bureaucratic capacity to organize it.

The fate of our times is characterized by rationalization and intellectualization and, above all, by the disenchantment of the world. The ultimate and most sublime values have retreated from public life.
Max Weber — Science as a Vocation, 1917

Horkheimer made the political economy connection explicit:

Whoever is not willing to talk about capitalism should keep quiet about fascism.
Horkheimer — The Jews and Europe, 1939

The argument: capitalism's disembedding of markets from social relations (Polanyi's point) created the mass dislocation, atomization, and loss of community that fascism then exploited. Capitalism produced atomized, disoriented individuals stripped of meaningful belonging — and fascism offered them the intoxicating substitute of national rebirth. The rationalist market created the wound. The irrational nation provided a counterfeit cure.

Hannah Arendt added the final, most disturbing piece. Adolf Eichmann, the architect of the logistics of the Holocaust, was not a monster. He was a bureaucrat — conscientious, efficient, proud of his organizational competence. He had, in Arendt's phrase, an "almost total inability to look at anything from the other fellow's point of view." He was, in short, a man of purely instrumental reason. A red button man, scaled to state power.

Adorno & Horkheimer
Dialectic of Enlightenment, 1944

Enlightenment reason has turned against itself. The same rationality that promised liberation produced fascism. Reason became instrumental — purely about efficiency and control, stripped of any substantive moral content.

Max Weber
The iron cage

Rational bureaucracy produces disenchantment: a world so thoroughly organized by instrumental rationality that no values outside it remain capable of judging or resisting it. God, tradition, community obligation — all dissolved.

Herbert Marcuse
One-Dimensional Man, 1964

Advanced capitalism produces a person who can only conceive of freedom as consumer choice — one for whom the red button is not just rational but the only choice that feels real. The one-dimensional man.

Enlightenment, understood in the widest sense as the advance of thought, has always aimed at liberating human beings from fear and installing them as masters. Yet the fully enlightened earth radiates disaster triumphant.
Adorno & Horkheimer — Dialectic of Enlightenment, 1944
A comfortable, smooth, reasonable, democratic unfreedom prevails in advanced industrial civilization, a token of technical progress.
Marcuse — One-Dimensional Man, 1964
The deepest claim

Instrumental rationalism is parasitic on social trust it did not create and steadily erodes. Markets require contracts to be honored, promises kept — not because it is always individually rational to do so, but because people were shaped by traditions that instilled these dispositions. As those traditions are dissolved by the market, the substrate markets depend on disappears too. The invisible hand was always borrowing against a moral capital it did not know how to replenish.

The blue button vindicated

56% pressed blue. Blue is, from a strictly instrumental rationalist standpoint, the wrong answer. You are gambling your survival on strangers you cannot coordinate with. The red button is sitting there with a guarantee. And most people ignored it.

But before treating blue as simply irrational, it is worth asking a harder question: is the rationalist case for red actually as airtight as it appears?

The crack in the rationalist argument

The red button's "moral out" — everyone reasons correctly, everyone presses red, everyone survives — depends on a premise that rationalism itself cannot guarantee: that everyone will in fact reason correctly. In the real poll, 56% pressed blue. Which means the rationalist who presses red knowing others will press blue is not participating in a coordination success — they are defecting on a blue-pressing majority that is actively trying to save everyone.


Put differently: if there is even a small probability that some people press blue, the moral calculus for red changes entirely. You are no longer the rational participant in a universal red-pressing consensus. You are the person who knew some would cooperate and chose not to join them. The invisible hand vanishes. What remains is free-riding on other people's virtue.

Q.
Can we guarantee everyone presses red?

No. Rationalism cannot guarantee coordination. People have different information, different reasoning, and — as the poll shows — different moral orientations. The universal red-pressing scenario that gives red its moral out is a fiction. In the actual world, some people always press blue.

Q.
If even one person presses blue, should we all?

Not necessarily — but the frame shifts. If a significant portion will press blue regardless, the question becomes: do you join the coalition trying to reach 50%, or do you defect on it? Pressing red in a world where blue is live is no longer the invisible hand. It is, precisely, free-riding.

Habermas offers one way to understand the 56%. Against instrumental reason he posited communicative reason: rationality oriented not toward individual optimization but toward mutual understanding. Where instrumental reason asks "what is my best move?", communicative reason asks "what could we all agree to?" The blue button is an attempt at the second question in a situation that formally allows only the first.

But the more interesting explanation may not be rationalist at all. The blue pressers may have been acting from something older — a moral intuition about what kind of person one should be, prior to calculation. Not "blue maximizes my expected outcome" but "I am not the kind of person who defects on a collective good when I can be the one to hold it together." This is virtue ethics in Aristotle's sense. It is also what Polanyi meant by embedded social relations: before markets abstracted economic life into price signals, exchange was embedded in obligation, reciprocity, and community expectation. You did not calculate whether to keep your word with your neighbor.

The open question

The Frankfurt School was pessimistic about whether these instincts could survive. The 56% suggests they are more resilient than Marcuse feared — or at least that they have not been fully dissolved yet. But the poll cannot tell us whether that number is stable. The Enlightenment rationalist system keeps producing red button people, and every generation inherits slightly less of the moral substrate that makes blue feel obvious. Whether the 56% holds over time is an open question.

A caveat about the question itself

The way the poll was worded creates a bias worth acknowledging. "If fewer than 50% press blue, only red voters survive" — this framing makes pressing red feel defensive, a hedge against the possibility that others won't cooperate. It carries an implicit threat. Consider a neutral reframing of the same logical situation:

neutral reframing

You are guaranteed to survive if you press red. If more than 50% press blue, those who press blue also survive.

The logical structure is identical. But the gut response shifts. Red is no longer a defensive move — it is simply the obvious, default choice. Blue becomes something you choose at personal risk on behalf of others. The 56% blue result would almost certainly not hold under this framing. More people would press red, and they would not feel guilty about it.

This matters because it means the poll doesn't cleanly measure solidarity against self-interest. It also measures how people respond to threat framing — and threat framing activates protective instincts that look like solidarity but may not be. The philosophical argument still stands, but its evidential basis needs this caveat.

The limits of the analogy

The red button is an unusually clean thought experiment because it keeps self-interest and collective interest perfectly separated. Universal red-pressing genuinely saves everyone. No one is harmed by your choice to press red, regardless of what anyone else does. This is what makes it philosophically interesting — and what makes it an imperfect model for real social decisions.

In most actual collective action problems, pressing red does impose costs on others. Someone goes without medical care because you opted out of the common pool. Someone's neighbourhood floods because emissions weren't collectively curtailed. The red button abstracts this away. In the real world, defection is never truly costless to those around you. The poll isolates the question of solidarity from the question of harm — but real life doesn't offer that isolation.

What it means in society

In actual society, there is a category of person for whom universal red-pressing doesn't work. The elderly person who can no longer participate in the labour market. The child born into poverty with no assets to leverage. The person with a disability that prevents economic independence. The person between jobs, between diagnoses, between stable housing. These are not edge cases — they are a structural feature of how market economies operate.

For these people, the question is not abstract. If the social order is organized entirely around individual rational self-interest — if everyone presses red in the extended sense of opting out of collective provision — they do not survive, or they survive very badly. They are structurally in the blue position: dependent on a sufficient number of others pressing blue to make it through. Their survival is a coordination problem they cannot solve alone.

The social safety net is the political institutionalization of blue-button logic. It tries to solve the coordination problem the poll poses — getting enough people to effectively press blue to keep the collective intact — without relying on the moral spontaneity of strangers. It makes blue the default, structured into law and funded through compulsion, so that collective provision doesn't depend on whether 56% happen to feel generous on a given day.

The political consequence

This is where Marcuse's one-dimensional man stops being a cultural observation and becomes a political one. The person capitalism has structurally produced — who can only think in terms of individual rational self-interest, for whom solidarity feels irrational — is also the constituency for dismantling the institutions that press blue on behalf of people with no other recourse. The Frankfurt School's warning was not that this would feel wrong to those people. It was that it would feel obvious.


Polanyi called this the double movement: market expansion produces social dislocation, which produces a protective counter-movement — welfare states, labour protections, collective insurance — as society tries to defend itself from the full force of market logic. The welfare state is not a moral gift from the well-off to the poor. It is the political answer, worked out over generations of conflict, to the question the red button poses: what do you do about the people for whom pressing red is not an option?
